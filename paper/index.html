A working paper

True, False, or Never Even Claimed

How Claim Validator validates a large language model's output — and what it still misses.

Independent claim validation service · September 2026 (updated)

This paper exists to give as honest an account of this tool as its own verdicts try to give of a claim — what it does, what it still gets wrong, and what changed since the last time this was written down. A claim, throughout, means any single checkable statement — a requirement, a summary sentence, a row in a spreadsheet — regardless of who or what produced it. For the API itself — every endpoint, a full quick-start walkthrough, and how to actually generate a report — see this project's own manual, docs/manual.html, not this paper.

01

Origins

It started as frustration with a small number, not a big idea.

An LLM generating claims about a document gets it wrong in a small minority of cases — by rough experience, on the order of 5% or less. That sounds like a solved problem until it's the thing you're actually staking on: a low error rate isn't a zero one, and there are only two ways to live with the difference. Read back over everything the model produced, by hand, every time — which quietly erases the reason for using the model at all. Or don't, and accept that the handful of wrong claims a run happens to contain will sit undetected in whatever consumes them next, compounding into cascading, harder-to-trace errors further down the pipeline. Neither is a real answer.

The point of the LLM was to not do the work by hand. Re-checking its output by hand, in full, every time, is the same work — it just moved.

DeepEval was the first thing tried, and it does answer the right question — is this text actually grounded in the source. What it doesn't give is a way in when the answer is no. A Faithfulness score tells you that a response scored low; pinpointing which specific statement was the problem, and why, from the number alone, is genuinely hard. That gap — a verdict with nothing behind it to inspect — is the specific thing this tool was built to close: not a replacement metric, but claim-level verdicts that each point back at the exact passage they're based on, so a low result is a place to start reading, not a dead end.

The judging core that does this — the part that reads a document, builds a structured map of what it's about, and decides whether a piece of text is actually backed by it — did not start as a validator at all. It began as a private, general-purpose research pipeline for discovering an ontology inside an arbitrary document: whatever concept types the document itself contains, extracted per-document rather than assumed in advance, then indexed for retrieval and checked with an LLM-as-judge entailment step. Claim Validator reuses that pipeline as a library rather than rewriting it, and everything genuinely new sits in a thin layer around it: an async job/API service, a set of shims that let a bare id + text claim satisfy contracts the underlying judge was never written to expect, and an Excel/JSON reporting layer.

02

What actually happens to a claim

One document, a bare list of claims, and two independent questions asked about them — never conflated into one score.

Two things are worth naming about that diagram. First, the ontology is built once and reused — the same document resubmitted under a different name, or no name, is caught by content hash and never re-indexed. Second, claims never touch the judge directly: they're retrieved against first, so a verdict is always anchored to a specific cited passage a human can go back and re-read, not a bare model opinion.

03

Four verdicts, not a score

A single 1–5 groundedness number can't distinguish a fabrication from an unaddressed claim. This tool refuses to collapse them.

entails

The cited passage actually supports the claim.

Working as intended — nothing to do.

contradicts

The cited passage says something that conflicts with the claim.

The most actionable finding this tool produces.

mentions_only

An on-topic passage exists, but stays silent on the specific thing claimed.

Not automatically wrong — often over-specific. Worth a look.

no_evidence

Nothing in the document supports or contradicts it at all.

A plausible-sounding claim the document never makes.

Every claim is judged three separate times; the reported verdict is whichever the judge agreed with itself on most. Disagreement across those three runs is itself a signal, kept alongside the verdict rather than thrown away in an average.

04

What it needs to know, and what it deliberately doesn't ask

"Requirements" were the first use case, not a boundary. What the engine takes as input — and refuses to take — says as much about its design as the verdicts do.

Any claim, not just requirements

The shape-check module this project reuses was originally built for software requirements, and its default rule demands something requirement-shaped: a named subject, an operation like GET /projects. Claim-validator's own layer overrides that outright — its rule set explicitly sets require_subject: False, with the reasoning stated directly in the code: a claim just needs non-trivial, checkable text, nothing else. A submitted claim is exactly two required fields, id and text — nothing else about its shape is assumed.

This isn't theoretical. Across the two rounds of testing in this paper, the same engine validated software requirements (Petstore, gRPC, WAI-ARIA), legal claims about a license (Apache), compliance claims about an internet standard (RFC 2119), and plain factual claims lifted from public-health guidance (CDC, WHO) — none of which are "requirements" in any software sense. Same pipeline, same four verdicts, no configuration needed to switch domains.

No field for who or what produced a claim

The one provenance field a claim can carry is source_ref — free text like "chatbot response, sentence 2." Its own docstring says exactly what it's for: "purely a pass-through... never read by validation logic, only carried into the report." There's no field for which model wrote a claim, what version, or what prompt produced it. Retrieval, the shape check, and the judge only ever see .text.

No field for intent, either — and that's on purpose

There's also no way to tell the engine, on a validation request, "these are requirements an LLM generated from an API spec" — no intent, genre, or origin field exists on POST /api/validations at all. Two adjacent, easy-to-conflate levers do exist, and are worth telling apart from that:

  • Background description, but only at ontology build time (POST /api/ontologies) — a domain seed for what concept types get discovered when the document is first read, nothing more. It never reaches the judge and has no effect on any claim's verdict.
  • Shape-rule overrides, per validation job, for a caller who genuinely wants stricter pre-checks — requiring a named subject again, say, because these really are structured software requirements and that structure is worth enforcing before a claim is even judged. Still only the free, deterministic pre-check; still never the entailment judge itself.

Nothing about a claim's declared purpose changes how it gets judged. That's not a missing feature — it's the same principle behind the missing provenance field, applied one level further: the entailment judge is supposed to be blind to who wrote a claim and why, so it can't be talked into trusting one more because it arrived framed as coming from a careful process. It reads the claim, reads the document, and answers the same question every time.

05

Correct is not the same as complete

The gap report asks a document-shaped question no per-claim verdict can answer: what did nobody even try to check?

Independently of judging any claim, the tool runs its own exhaustive census of the document's concept instances — the things it discovered the document is actually about — and reports which of those no submitted claim's citation ever touched. It's deliberately kept apart from the per-claim verdicts: a claim set can be 100% entails and still leave a gap report showing most of the document was never engaged with at all.

06

One tenant, one silo — sharing as little as possible

The judging mechanism in the last section runs the same way regardless of who's calling it. Who can reach whose data is a separate question, answered at the infrastructure level, not the application level.

Every deployment is a silo: one Container App, one database, one Key Vault holding that tenant's own Anthropic key and bearer token, one storage share, per tenant. Nothing about one tenant's data or spend is reachable through another tenant's credentials. Two tenants share exactly three things, and none of them carry tenant data: the Container Apps compute environment, the Postgres server (each tenant gets its own separate database on it, not a shared one), and the image registry. A fourth shared resource, the audit-log workspace, does carry activity records from both tenants — but only operation metadata (what was read, written, or deleted, and when), never document content or secrets.

None of that isolation runs on passwords. Each Container App gets its own Azure-managed identity — a credential nothing types in and nothing can leak, because there's no secret value to begin with — and every connection it makes is a specific, named RBAC role assignment scoped to exactly one resource: Key Vault Secrets User on its own vault, AcrPull on the shared registry, and its own Postgres role, granted through a single shared identity (pg-admin-identity) that exists only to do the granting at setup time and is never used at runtime by either tenant. There is no Postgres admin password anywhere in this project — not in Key Vault, not in a Container App's config, not in the Bicep templates that provision any of it.

A full Entra ID (Azure AD) per-tenant authentication mode exists too — app roles, a service principal, role assignment via Microsoft Graph — but stays deliberately optional. The default is simpler on purpose: deploy it yourself, call it with a bearer token, no centrally managed identity provider standing between a user and their own deployment. Entra ID stays available for whoever wants real per-user job privacy.

Within one tenant, jobs and reports are scoped further still, down to the individual caller (owner_user_id on the Job row) — real per-person privacy when that tenant is configured for Entra ID auth, or one shared identity for everyone holding the same bearer token under the default shared-secret mode described above. Ontologies are the one thing genuinely shared within a tenant, not across tenants: built once, immutable, listed for anyone on that tenant to browse and reuse, never edited or deleted through the API — the same immutability this paper's evidence section relied on to reuse ontologies across two separate test rounds without rebuilding them.

None of this is aspirational. Both tenants shown here are real, running deployments — usera-claimval is the one every live result in this paper came from — scoped from a written Azure-readiness assessment before a single resource existed, then provisioned by three Bicep templates deployed in order (a one-time admin identity, the shared environment, then a template per tenant). The full account is in the project's own infra/README.md — kept as a real deployment history, not a clean-room design document.

07

What gets checked besides "is it true"

Before the judge ever runs, and after every verdict is in, two more layers ask questions the entailment check alone can't.

The shape check — free, before any model is called

Every claim is tested against a "does this even have enough here to check" rule before a single token is spent judging it: a subject, a title, at least one stated criterion. Deterministic, no LLM call involved, and nothing is ever deleted on the strength of it — a claim that fails is flagged, not dropped. A claim can be well-written and entirely true and still fail this check, because it states nothing checkable at all — a different failure mode from being wrong.

The edge case this check exists to catch: an ontology filing an OpenAPI tag as if it were an endpoint. The generated requirements about them look well-formed — each has a subject — so a check that only confirms a subject is present would pass every one, because a present subject isn't automatically a valid one. projects is a tag; GET /projects is an operation on it. The check instead pattern-matches what a subject actually looks like, not just whether one exists.

The quality sheet — after every claim is judged

Every finished report carries an aggregate Quality tab, and every metric on it is sorted into one of four buckets by what a person should do about it next — the same triage logic the verdicts themselves use, applied to the report as a whole:

08 Shipped — live-tested

Reading the passage, not just the reason

A verdict is only as checkable as what's next to it. Until this shipped, "next to it" was a paraphrase and a number that meant nothing on its own.

Every verdict already carries a reason — the judge's own account of what it saw — and cited_chunks, the index numbers of whichever passages retrieval pulled up. The reason is usually a fair summary. The index numbers were never independently useful: neither the JSON result nor the Excel report exposed the actual passage text behind them, so confirming a verdict for real meant either trusting the paraphrase or going to search the source document by hand, with nothing but a bare integer to start from.

Before

"cited_chunks": [2, 0, 1, 3]

"reason": "The passages state 'expires_in'... directly contradicting the claim"

Four numbers with nothing to resolve them against, and a paraphrase you either trust or don't.

Now

"cited_passages": ["...\"expires_in\": Integer... Token lifetime in seconds...", "...Token Expiration (3600 second TTL)..."]

The actual sentences the judge read, in the same order as the indices — the same passage a human would land on searching the source by hand, already in front of them.

The fix stayed inside a real boundary: no free-text "ask the document a question" endpoint, and there won't be one. Considered directly and rejected — the point is a human proofreading the real source, not trusting a second AI-generated answer instead of the first claim. Surfacing the passage the judge already retrieved isn't a query feature; it's exposing computation that had already happened, one step earlier than it used to stop.

Confirmed live, not just designed: resubmitted a claim already documented earlier in this paper — "Authentication tokens never expire once issued" — against the same live tenant. It still landed on contradicts, exactly as before, but this time the result carried the actual sentences behind that call: "expires_in": Integer ... Token lifetime in seconds and Token Expiration (3600 second TTL) — the real passage a person would otherwise have had to go find themselves.

Free, by construction: the passage text was already loaded in memory at the exact point a verdict gets built — retrieval had already pulled it to hand the judge something to read. Surfacing it added no LLM call, no latency, no new cost; it's the same data, just no longer thrown away after the judge finished with it. 225 tests passing (from 219), verified against a real downloaded report on the live tenant before shipping.

09 Shipped — live-tested

More than one document, one ontology

A reference document was never actually limited to one file — a caller just had nowhere to see which of several it was reading from. Both halves are covered here: the combining, which already existed, and the attribution, which didn't.

An ontology has never been tied to a single file. The engine already treats a document upload as a "document set" — its own content-hash function is named exactly that, hashing every file's bytes together, order-independent, so the same set of files resubmitted in any order still hits the cache. Every document in the set gets chunked and merged into one combined retrieval index, and one unified list of concept types gets discovered across all of them together, not one ontology per file. A caller reaches this by uploading each related document separately, then passing every resulting file path together in one files list to a single ontology or validation request — several regulatory documents, a spec plus its amendments, a policy and its appendices, checked as the one thing they actually are.

What was missing was attribution. A claim's citation already carried the retrieved passage text (previous section), but with more than one document behind an ontology, nothing said which file that passage came from. Each retrieved chunk already tracked its own source file internally — a real, working, already-tested method nobody was calling from the API layer. Fixed the same way the passage text itself was: expose data that was already being computed, add nothing new to compute.

Confirmed live: two documents with nothing in common — an API specification and a page of standard poker hand rankings — uploaded separately, then combined into one ontology in a single call. The build found seven concept types spanning both. A claim about the rate limit correctly cited the API spec; claims about poker hands correctly cited the rankings page — each citation now naming its actual source file, not just an index number meaningless without one.

The same discipline as everywhere else in this section: the common case stays exactly as it was. A citation from a single-document ontology — every real result shown earlier in this paper — renders identically to before, no filename clutter on the normal case. The source only appears once a claim's own citations genuinely span more than one document, which is precisely the situation where knowing which one stops being optional. 229 tests passing (from 225), verified against a real downloaded report from the two-document run before shipping.

10 Shipped — live-tested

The shape check learns the document

The shape check (section 05) asks one deliberately narrow question by default: does a claim have enough text to be worth checking at all. The underlying engine it's built on can ask a sharper, domain-shaped question — does this claim name a real, specific thing, not just a topic — but that capability was inert for claim-validator's own bare claims, because nothing supplied the "subject" a domain-aware rule would check against.

How it actually works: the document teaches the rule, once

Rather than hand-writing a rule file per domain, an LLM proposes one — a single time per document, at the point the ontology is first built (or backfilled onto one that already exists). It reads the concept types Phase 1 already discovered and proposes a rule in the exact shape the shape-check engine expects: which fields a claim needs, and — where the domain has one — a pattern for what a real "subject" looks like there.

The model's proposal is never trusted verbatim. The object the shape check actually inspects for a bare claim exposes almost nothing domain-specific — no populated "subject," an always-empty criteria list — so a proposal to require a subject would flag every claim unconditionally, not selectively. The inference step forces that setting off, unconditionally, regardless of what the model asks for, and drops a proposed ID-pattern rule the same way, since a claim's ID is caller-chosen, not something the document determines. Every one of these corrections is logged, not silent.

The model is spent once, to discover structure — not once per claim, to render an opinion. The generated rule then applies exactly as deterministically as the static default it replaces, cached alongside the ontology and reused on every later validation against that document, at the same zero marginal cost the ontology itself already enjoys.

Eleven documents, real deployment, zero deviations

Redeployed to the same live tenant this paper's evidence section ran against, then tested twice: once backfilling profiles onto the seven ontologies already built for this paper and re-validating each against its new profile, and once more against four new documents chosen to stress a different edge each — a larger, multi-operation API spec; a near-empty document, to see what happens with almost nothing to work from; a French-language document, to check the pipeline handles non-English text and accented characters cleanly end to end; and a structurally unrelated domain — standard poker hand rankings — with no API-like or legal-like shape at all.

Findings from actually running it, not just designing it:

  • require_subject stayed False by default, every time — but that's a different, weaker claim than "the clamp fired." Across all eleven real documents' production runs, the model was never once asked the loaded question — the shipped prompt only ever asks a softer, informational one ("would a subject check make sense here?"), on purpose, precisely so it never invites the dangerous key. So require_subject being False in all eleven real profiles is honestly a mix of "never proposed" and "correctly forced off" — and it's worth being precise about which, since the two read very differently.
  • Checked separately, with a real model, whether the clamp itself actually works — and it does. Using a research-only prompt variant that directly asked a real model "should require_subject be true here," against a purpose-written strict RPC contract and the real, already-built Petstore ontology: the model said true both times — a real, live model genuinely wants stricter enforcement on a document built for exactly that. The raw response was then fed, unmodified, into the actual shipped clamping code (not a reimplementation) — which forced require_subject back to False and logged the expected explanation both times. The clamp isn't just correct in a mock; it's confirmed to survive contact with a real model that actually disagrees with it.
  • A minor rough edge, found only by that same test — since fixed: the shipped prompt gates whether a proposed subject_pattern gets kept at all behind a separate field (an informational "would a subject check make sense" flag) that this research prompt never asked about — so in that one test, a perfectly valid pattern the model offered was silently dropped rather than logged as dropped. Harmless either way (the pattern is inert while enforcement stays off), but it broke this module's own rule that every correction gets explained. Fixed directly: that drop path now logs a note too, the same as the invalid-regex path already did.
  • Confirmed live, not just via the in-process test client: a bad regex gets a clean 422 from the real deployed server, and a fresh document built for the first time through the real HTTP API (not a local script) gets an automatic profile exactly like the design intends. And a per-request override deliberately re-enabling require_subject was confirmed to correctly beat an ontology's own inferred profile on a live call — the one claim it was tested against came back exactly one shape violation, precisely because the override worked.

Where this still stops working — unchanged by any of this

Not every domain has a syntax worth checking, and nothing in this run changes that boundary. An API spec, an RFC's "MUST/SHOULD" vocabulary, a legal citation format — these have real, learnable shapes a regex can catch for free. A domain whose interesting structure is genuinely about meaning — "does this claim distinguish an indication from a contraindication" — isn't a shape question, it's a judgment question, and answering it would mean reintroducing a second judge earlier in the pipeline. None of the eleven documents tested here were chosen to test that boundary specifically; it remains an open, named limitation rather than one anything above has closed.

Where the profile actually shows up

Not a silent setting, on any of the eleven runs above: the applied rule and its source ("default" / "llm" / "llm_failed") are logged next to main_model/judge_model on every report's Quality tab, exposed on GET /api/ontologies/{key}, and summarized on the shared ontology listing — confirmed present, with real explanatory text, not the generic "undocumented metric" fallback, on every Excel report downloaded during this test.

11 Shipped — live-tested

Three structural corrections, layered on top of the judge

Three edge cases the judge's own reasoning didn't handle, each surfaced by running the tool against real documents rather than by design review — each now handled structurally, outside the judge entirely.

A deterministic check for one specific numeric failure

The judge sometimes compares a claim's number to a document's threshold literally — "3% is not 0.1%, so contradicts" — instead of asking which side of the threshold 3% actually falls on. Three rounds of prompt engineering narrowed this without closing it: the model can walk the right procedure and still override itself at the last step. Code that does the arithmetic can't. A new deterministic module recognizes exactly one shape — a claim stating one number and one binary outcome word, checked against a cited passage stating a comparison operator, a number, and the same kind of outcome word — and overrides the judge's verdict only when its own arithmetic says the judge got it wrong. Everything outside that one shape (two or more numbers, a spelled-out count, an ambiguous outcome word) is left to the judge untouched, by design, not as a shortcut.

Three live bugs closed while hardening it, each found by running it against a real brief rather than by inspection: a bolded number in a source document ("more than **0.1%**") broke the operator-to-number adjacency match; a markdown heading with no terminal punctuation merged into the next sentence and tripled the apparent outcome-word count; and the word "passed" turned out to mean both "a check passed" and "a flag was passed" with no way to tell those senses apart from the word alone, so it was removed from the vocabulary entirely.

A fourth edge case surfaced later, not while hardening this module but while testing an unrelated fix below: the checker false-contradictsed a claim that simply restated the document's own threshold rule — "it fails a page when more than 0.1% of its pixels differ from the baseline," a verbatim-correct echo of the brief's own sentence — because the extractor treated the claim's own number as a reported instance value rather than recognizing it sat inside the claim's own comparison phrase. Handled the same way every other edge case in this module is handled: abstain rather than guess, this time specifically when the claim's own number is itself preceded by a recognized comparator phrase. Verified live: the exact reproduction flipped from contradicts (wrong) to entails (correct), with zero change to the ordinary instance-value case the module was built for.

Reconciling two names for the same thing, without inventing new machinery

The gap report's own fuzzy name matching — stemming plus token overlap, threshold 0.5 — has a documented, measured blind spot: two independently-named passes over the same rule scored 0.286 on a real case, below threshold, while a genuinely unrelated pair scored 0.111, too close to safely lower the bar and catch one without catching the other. The fix reused, rather than rebuilt, a batched LLM-judged name-reconciliation function already proven in production elsewhere in this codebase — wired into the gap report as an additive fallback tier that only runs on names the free matcher already gave up on, wrapped in the same try/except-degrades-gracefully pattern its original call site already used. A new ambiguous reason distinguishes "found real candidates, couldn't confirm the pairing" from "no evidence anywhere" — a materially different, softer finding the previous two-reason scheme had no way to say.

Widening retrieval for a claim that states two facts at once

Retrieval sends one query per claim. A claim asserting two independent facts risks the more prominent fact's own vocabulary crowding out the other's in that one shared query — the real, previously-unaddressed CDC handwashing finding this paper's own evidence section documents (a compound claim verified on only half its content) is exactly this failure. The fix detects a claim that looks syntactically compound — a comma or semicolon before a coordinating "and," never one found inside a quoted string or a code span — and, only then, retrieves separately for each clause and unions the results into the claim's own citation set. The claim and its verdict are never split; only what evidence the one verdict gets to see is widened. A non-compound claim, the ordinary case, is retrieved exactly as before — zero behavior change, zero added cost, no new LLM call anywhere in any of these three fixes.

Live verification of the last two fixes: deployed against real tenants and re-run against every example document available, both mechanisms cost nothing and changed nothing on documents small enough that a single query already covers the whole thing.
12 Shipped — live-tested

Validating a spec generator's own output

OpenSpec generates requirement specs from a brief — which makes its output exactly the kind of unowned claim this tool exists to check, not an exception to needing it.

OpenSpec is a convention layer over spec-driven development, not a validator itself: a capability spec is a ## Purpose plus a set of ### Requirement blocks, each carrying SHALL/MUST language and one or more #### Scenario blocks stating a WHEN/THEN/AND condition. A team points OpenSpec at a source brief; an LLM proposes the requirement specs. Nothing about that generation step is checked by OpenSpec itself — which is precisely the gap this tool's own architecture already exists to close for any other LLM output, so the natural question was whether it closes this one too, without OpenSpec and claim-validator needing to know anything about each other.

The answer is a small, deliberately separate adapter — tools/openspec-adapter/ — that parses an OpenSpec capability spec structurally (section-walking and pattern matching, no model call) into this project's own bare {id, text, source_ref} claim shape, at requirement, scenario, or individual-assertion granularity. Kept out of claim-validator's own package on purpose: zero imports of claimvalidator, phases, or the database, its own test suite and its own pytest.ini, usable or discarded independently of everything else in this repository. Deliberately not exposed as an API, either — the parsing step itself is deterministic and cheap enough to run as a local script wherever an OpenSpec repository already lives, and the only place an LLM enters this whole workflow is OpenSpec's own generation step, upstream of the adapter entirely and outside what claim-validator has any reason to wrap.

The resulting workflow answers a specific, previously informal question with the same rigor this tool already applies everywhere else: OpenSpec generates requirements from a brief; the adapter converts those requirements into claims; claim-validator checks each one against the same brief it was generated from — repeatably, and re-runnable on demand, rather than trusted once by eye.

An edge case surfaced live testing the adapter against its own examples rather than by inspection: OpenSpec's WHEN-folding occasionally inverted a scenario's own sequencing bullets — a "capture the screenshot" step and an "only then close the tap" step joined into one claim in the wrong order, the claim asserting the steps happen in an order the spec never states. The adapter now detects an ordering-prefix ("only then," "after that") and preserves the sequence it signals, rather than flattening every WHEN/AND bullet into one undifferentiated list.

Two seeded example projects — an API-testing capability spec and a UI-testing capability spec, each with deliberately planted errors against its own source brief — exercise the adapter and the validator together end to end, and reviewing their own answer-key tables surfaced a second necessary correction before either was ever used to grade a run: the UI-testing example's README originally marked two out-of-scope items as expected no_evidence, when an explicit exclusion actively contradicts a claim asserting the item is in scope — corrected to contradicts before being trusted as a reference table. The same discipline this paper applies to the tool's own output — check the answer key as carefully as the answer — applied here to a fixture nobody had reason to doubt until it was run against something real.

gRPC helloworld.proto & WHO food-safety fact sheet entails

12 of 12, and separately 40 of 40, claims entailed — no contradictions, no gaps, on two completely unrelated documents.

Included deliberately: a validator that only ever finds something wrong isn't independent, it's contrarian. Clean passes on two well-written sources are themselves evidence the tool isn't manufacturing findings.

In plain terms: the same mistake — writing something as a plain fact when the source only ever states it as a condition or a recommendation — showed up twice, in two fields that share nothing (a web-accessibility spec and an internet standard), made by two different models. It wasn't a one-off fluke tied to one odd document or one weaker model — the second time, it was the strongest model tested that made it, which is what makes it worth trusting as a real, recurring failure mode rather than noise. And on the two documents where the generated claims genuinely had nothing wrong with them, the tool said exactly that — full marks, nothing invented. A checker that always finds something wrong isn't really checking anything; the clean passes are what make the flagged ones credible.

An eighth document, run the other way round. Every row above is a model-generated claim set, checked for whatever it happened to get wrong. The question here isn't "what does it catch" but "does it land where a careful reader decided it should."

OpenSpec cli-list spec · claims hand-authored as a labelled set · judged by Haiku 14 / 16

The two that diverged were both boundary calls, not wrong-direction errors — nothing false was called entails, and no real contradiction was missed.

Divergence one, arguably the tool's point: a claim that a count is rendered "as a percentage bar" when the spec states it as plain text ("requirements 12") — the answer key said mentions_only, the tool said contradicts. A stated format and a different asserted format genuinely conflict; the tool's read is defensible, maybe better than the key's. Divergence two, the genuinely thin seam: a claim about a --json flag the document never mentions — the key said no_evidence, the tool split 2/3 and returned mentions_only, anchoring on the document's output-format requirement as an on-topic passage that stays silent on the flag. The two compound claims, built to fail, didn't. The CDC finding above shows a compound claim splitting on its least-retrieved half. Here one claim bundled four separately-checkable assertions, all true; another bundled three, with a single false clause set between two true ones. Both came back correct 3/3 — the false-clause one with that clause named in the judge's reason ("Task progress is for changes mode only"). The compound weakness is real but not universal: whether retrieval surfaces every part is what decides it.

Changing the model — and why one anchored judge beats two compared ones

The model doing the work is a setting, not an identity. What matters more than which model you pick is what role it's playing, and what it's being checked against.

What actually happens when you swap the model

Which model runs is an environment variable on the deployed tenant — CLAIMVAL_PROVIDER / CLAIMVAL_MODEL — resolved through the same tiering the rest of the project uses, never a hardcoded id. Left unset, as it is on both currently deployed tenants, it falls back to the cheapest available tier — Haiku. Two roles are configurable independently, even though the deployed service doesn't currently split them: main_model, which builds the ontology and retrieves passages, and judge_model, which actually renders each verdict — the pipeline supports a cheaper judge sitting behind a stronger extractor, or vice versa. What a model swap never changes is the shape of the output: the same four verdicts, the same citation requirement, the same 3-run majority vote. It changes how good the answers are, not what's being asked — which is also why every report logs both model names on its Quality tab: comparing two runs without first checking whether the model changed between them is exactly the mistake that logging exists to catch.

The generator and the judge were never the same model here

Worth stating plainly, because the evidence section above makes it easy to miss: nothing that wrote a claim in this paper was the same process that judged it. Claim generation — Haiku, Sonnet, or Opus, chosen deliberately per document — happened in a separate script outside claim-validator entirely, because claim-validator doesn't generate claims, by design. Every one of those claim sets was then judged by the deployed service's own configured model, which was Haiku, its unconfigured default, for every single run in this set. That means two of the seven documents — RFC 2119, written by Opus; CDC guidance, written by Sonnet — were checked by a smaller, cheaper model than the one that had written the claims under test, and the cheaper model still caught a real error in both. That's not an accident of this particular run. It's the actual argument for the architecture: the judge doesn't need to be as capable as the generator, because it isn't generating anything — it only has to compare one specific sentence to one specific retrieved passage and say whether they agree.

Why this beats comparing two judges against each other

A common alternative pattern runs two different judge models over the same output and treats disagreement between them as the signal — if Model A and Model B don't agree, something's probably wrong. It reads like independent verification. Usually it isn't, for two reasons this architecture avoids on purpose:

Two judges, compared

Disagreement tells you the two models saw it differently — not which one is right, or why. There's no ground truth inside the comparison itself, only two guesses measured against each other.

Agreement isn't reassurance either. Models trained on overlapping data can share the same blind spot and confidently agree on the same wrong answer — correlated mistakes look exactly like consensus.

One judge, anchored to the source

Every verdict is checked against a specific passage retrieved from the actual reference document — not against another model's opinion. The "second opinion" is the document itself, not a second guesser.

Repeating the same judge three times measures whether one model's own reading of this one passage holds up under retry — a check on noise, not a vote between two personalities.

The one place a second model does get involved is escalation: when three runs can't agree, or a contradiction only holds by a bare 2-of-3 majority, a stronger model re-judges — against the very same cited passage, not a fresh independent guess — and even that second opinion is checked for its own internal consensus rather than trusted on a single pass. It's a targeted appeal to a better judge on the rare cases actually worth the extra cost, not a standing panel of two models graded against each other on everything. And because every verdict, first-pass or escalated, ships with the exact passage it was based on, a disagreement is something a person can actually go read and resolve — not two bare scores with nothing behind them to compare.

What it doesn't do

Named plainly, because a tool that's vague about its own edges is harder to trust inside its actual ones.

  • It doesn't write claims. Validate-only, by design — feed it output from an LLM, a person, or any other tool. It never generates the thing it's checking.
  • It doesn't execute anything. No live system, no API call, no code path is run to check a claim — only the reference document, read as text.
  • There is no free-text "ask the document a question" endpoint, and none is planned. A deliberate scope boundary, not a gap: the point is a human proofreading the real source a verdict cites, not trusting a second AI-generated answer instead of the first claim. Every retrieval this engine does happens in service of judging a specific, caller-supplied claim — never as an open-ended query a caller poses directly.
  • It isn't a safety or content-moderation suite. No hate/violence/self-harm/jailbreak detection, no agent or tool-call evaluation. One job, done deliberately narrowly.
  • Its coverage check has a known blind spot. The gap report can occasionally list something as never-addressed when a claim did address it, if the document's own analysis named that instance differently in two internal passes with little shared vocabulary. A strong signal, documented as imperfect rather than certain.
  • Ontologies are immutable once built. No edit, no versioning, no delete route — a shared asset that anyone on a tenant can reuse can't also be silently changed or removed by any one caller.
  • Anthropic-only, on purpose. No OpenAI dependency anywhere in the pipeline, including at import time — a deliberate constraint, not an oversight.
  • The judge disagrees with itself, run to run. Its own code documents roughly 20–25% verdict variance across identical repeated calls — real enough that this project has used it, more than once, to correctly tell "this verdict changed because of a fix" apart from "this verdict changed because the judge is the judge" in its own regression testing.
  • The structural overrides are deliberately narrow, not general reasoning. The numeric-threshold checker (see Structural fixes, below) recognizes exactly one claim shape and abstains on everything else — two or more numbers, a spelled-out count, an ambiguous outcome word, or the claim's own number sitting inside a comparison phrase — by design, not as a shortcut to widen later.
  • The compound-claim splitter is asymmetric on purpose. It only treats a claim as compound when a comma or semicolon precedes a coordinating "and" — missing a genuinely compound claim written without that punctuation costs nothing (today's single-query behavior, unchanged); the risk deliberately runs toward under-splitting, never toward inventing a split that wasn't there.
  • The judge doesn't reliably catch a weakened quantifier. A claim that softens a document's "every"/"all" into "some"/"most" is, read strictly, not false — but it misses the real-world implicature a human reader would flag: stating "some" when the rule is "all, no exceptions" is misleading in practice. Reproduced on two unrelated documents and independently corroborated by an external tool (DeepEval) catching the same cases this project's own judge missed. Tracked, not resolved — open, and not currently being worked.
  • The line between "silent on this" and "doesn't mention this at all" is the judge's softest boundary. Across every benchmark round run against this project, mentions_only and no_evidence are confused with each other more than any other verdict pair — entails and contradicts are never confused with either. A judge-prompt fix closed part of this gap; one class of claim (an on-topic detail the document never confirms) still lands on the wrong side of it.

Where it sits next to the alternatives

Not the only way to check whether text is grounded in a source — the differences are in what you bring to it and what the answer looks like.

Against DeepEval

DeepEval

A Pytest-style library. You write test cases, choose metrics, run them in your own CI. Its Faithfulness/Hallucination metrics score against whatever context you hand them at eval time.

Claim Validator

A hosted, multi-tenant API. Post a document and bare id + text claims; it builds and caches its own ontology-grounded retrieval, then separately reports a document-wide coverage gap DeepEval's per-claim metrics never ask about.

The comparison above is a design argument. This is the live measurement behind it: DeepEval's FaithfulnessMetric, run externally against this project, fed the exact same cited passages Claim Validator itself retrieved for each claim — holding evidence constant so the result isolates judgment, not retrieval — against a 28-claim adversarial set with ground truth fixed before either system saw it.

vs. ground truth
Claim Validator25/28 (89.3%)
DeepEval23/28 (82.1%)
Agreement between the two20/28 (71.4%)

Claim Validator scores higher, but the more useful fact is that the two tools' misses never overlapped — every claim one got wrong, the other got right. DeepEval independently caught the same quantifier-shift weakening ("all" → "some") this project's own judge misses, corroborating that gap from an outside tool. Claim Validator, in turn, catches two things DeepEval's Faithfulness metric is not designed to: a half-true compound claim (DeepEval scores it 0.5, but its own pass/fail threshold rounds that up to "faithful"), and a claim that adds an unstated or fabricated specific (nothing in it contradicts the source, so a metric that only checks for contradiction has nothing to flag — the concrete, measured version of the gap named earlier in this section).

Cost was measured the same way — real Anthropic usage from both systems, not an estimate, on a small held-out document and six claims:

Claim Validator — ontology/RAG build (one-time, per document)23$0.0554

On a brand-new document, Claim Validator's first run costs about 3.5× DeepEval's — almost entirely the one-time ontology build. That index is then reused for every later claim checked against the same document, so once it exists, the marginal cost per claim is close to identical between the two (roughly $0.0036 for Claim Validator, $0.0037 for DeepEval) — Claim Validator's per-claim judging is not more expensive than DeepEval's, only its upfront indexing is. One caveat worth stating plainly: this measurement fed DeepEval retrieval it didn't have to build itself; a deployment where DeepEval also owns its own RAG setup would carry a retrieval cost this comparison doesn't count.

Against Microsoft Foundry's evaluators

Foundry

~35 orthogonal evaluators — quality, safety, agent behavior, RAG groundedness — assembled per project from an SDK. Its closest analogue, Groundedness, collapses to a 1–5 score or a pass/fail at 3. That number can't distinguish "this contradicts the source" from "this just isn't addressed" — they read the same.

Claim Validator

One narrow, opinionated pipeline. Four distinct verdicts on purpose, its own retrieval built in rather than supplied, and a document-wide coverage report no evaluator in Foundry's catalog attempts — at the cost of the breadth Foundry has and this tool deliberately doesn't.

Reach for Foundry when you're already governing a broad evaluation surface across many projects inside the Azure AI ecosystem. Reach for this tool when the job is narrower and concrete: a finished batch of claims about one document, needing an independent, auditable, four-way verdict — outside that ecosystem entirely, deployed into infrastructure you actually control.

Why this matters where being wrong is expensive

The moment an LLM's output about a contract, a clinical protocol, a regulatory standard, or an incident report is trusted enough to act on, something other than the model that wrote it has to check it.

A model that summarizes a license, drafts findings against a clinical guideline, or writes claims about a compliance document is exactly as trustworthy as whoever checks it next — and a human re-reading the source line by line doesn't scale, while asking the same model to grade its own homework answers a different, weaker question. What's needed in a critical-area setting isn't a single confidence number; it's an independent judge that never wrote the claim in the first place, that anchors every verdict to a specific, re-readable passage rather than a bare opinion, and that separates is this right from is this complete — because a regulator, a clinician, or a counterparty asking "did you check everything, or just the parts that were easy to check" deserves an actual answer, not a single blended score standing in for both questions at once.

That's also why the deployment model isn't incidental to the argument. A tool that only exists as someone else's shared cloud endpoint asks an organization handling sensitive documents to send them somewhere new. This one ships as infrastructure a tenant runs and controls entirely — its own Container App, its own database, its own Key Vault, its own audit log — reached with a token that tenant generated and can rotate on its own schedule. The independence that matters here isn't just "a different model did the judging." It's that the judging happens inside a boundary the document's owner drew themselves.

None of this requires the caller to be a person. Every endpoint here is plain REST, callable by a human tester or an AI agent alike.
